The four mad brahmins

A great feast had been announced in Kanchipuram in honour of the Brahmins, who were highly respected by everyone for their religious knowledge and wisdom.
Four Brahmins, each coming from a different village, met on the road leading there and decided to make the journey together.
As they walked along, they encountered a soldier travelling in the opposite direction.
The soldier greeted them respectfully, joining his hands together.
All four Brahmins responded at the same time with an appropriate blessing, and they went their separate ways.
When they reached a well, the Brahmins stopped for a while to drink and rest in the shade of a fig tree. One of them began to speak:
“ The soldier we met was a man of great discernment. Did you notice how politely he greeted me?”
“He wasn’t greeting you,” replied the Brahmin beside him. “He was greeting me!”
“You are both mistaken,” said the third. “His greeting was meant for me, and I can prove it: he was looking at me as he spoke.”
“Not at all,” said the fourth. “It was me he greeted so respectfully. Otherwise, why would I have returned his greeting?”
Their voices rose, and the argument grew so heated that they nearly came to blows.
Seeing this, one of them declared:
“Why are we getting so angry? We are Brahmins, not savages! Let us hurry after the soldier and find out which one of us he greeted.”
They all considered this very wise advice.
They began to run and finally caught up with the soldier, out of breath, some distance from where they had met him.
They explained the cause of their quarrel and asked him to settle their dispute.
Realising what sort of men he was dealing with, the soldier decided to have some fun at their expense.
“My greeting was intended for the greatest fool among you.”
Then he turned his back on them and continued on his way without another word.
The Brahmins resumed their journey, silent and thoughtful.
After a few hours, one of them could contain himself no longer. He insisted that the greeting must have been intended for him, since he was unquestionably the greatest fool of the four.
No sooner had he finished speaking than the others protested, each claiming the title of greatest fool for himself.
Once again, their voices rose, and the argument grew so heated that they nearly came to blows.
The Brahmin who had proposed a solution to their first quarrel addressed his companions:
“I know that I am more foolish than any of you, yet each of you claims to be more foolish than I am. What is the point of fighting? Let us put our quarrel aside. We are now close to the town of Walajabad. Let us go to the courthouse and ask a judge to settle our disagreement.”
This suggestion was immediately approved.
Having obtained permission to present their case, one of them recounted the entire affair, from the soldier’s greeting to their quarrel over which of the four was the greatest fool.
Throughout his account, the whole assembly burst into laughter several times. Delighted by this unexpected opportunity, the judge also decided to amuse himself at their expense. He assumed a serious expression and addressed the disputants:
“You are all four strangers in this town, and no witness can speak on your behalf. Let each of you, therefore, be his own witness and recount the incident in his life that best demonstrates his foolishness. We shall then decide which of you deserved the soldier’s greeting.”
All four disputants agreed to this proposal…

About This Tale:
This Indian folktale comes from stories collected and translated by Abbé Jean-Antoine Dubois (1765–1848), a French missionary who spent many years in southern India.
It belongs to the Indian tradition of humorous tales, passed down orally and often featuring characters renowned for their wisdom.
Its original author and date of origin are unknown.
