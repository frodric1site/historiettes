The four foolish Brahmins : The verdict

Here is the second Brahmin’s story:
One day, after having my head shaved, I told my wife to give the barber one sou in payment, but the scatterbrain gives him two.
I demand my sou back, but the barber refuses to listen.
The argument grows heated, insults begin to fly, and the barber offers me a deal:
— I cannot return your sou, but if you agree, I will shave your wife’s head instead.
The offer seems perfectly fair to me.
— Agreed!
My wife tries to run away, but I grab her and hold her down on the ground while the barber shaves her head.
Once it is done, she showers us with insults before running off as fast as her legs can carry her.
Word of my adventure spread, and some people could not resist adding that I had ordered my wife’s head to be shaved after catching her in the act of adultery…
Shortly afterwards, I attended a banquet.
The hundred or so Brahmins present pestered me to reveal the name of the guilty man.
I solemnly protested my wife’s innocence and explained how I had ordered her head to be shaved simply to avoid losing a sou.
— Why else would anyone shave a married woman’s head, if not for adultery? Either this man is lying, or he is the greatest fool on earth! exclaimed one of them.
The second Brahmin ended his story and looked at the gathering with a broad smile on his face.
Once the laughter had died down, the judge decided that this act of folly deserved consideration and invited the third Brahmin to tell his story…

Here it is:
My name used to be Anantaya, but everyone now calls me Betel-Anantaya.
A month ago, I told my wife that all women were chatterboxes. She replies that some men are even bigger chatterboxes than women. From the look in her eyes, I realise she is talking about me.
Confident of winning, I suggest that we stop talking and make a bet to see which of us will break the silence first. The loser will have to give the winner a betel leaf.
The next morning, when our neighbours do not see us come outside and nobody answers their calls, they break down our door and find us sitting there, eyes wide open, completely silent.
They conclude that some unknown enemy has cast a spell on us.
A Brahmin friend who happens to be there offers to cure us.
He has a small gold ingot brought to him and places it in a brazier. When it is glowing red-hot, he picks it up with tongs and presses it first against the soles of my feet, then above my knees, against my stomach and, finally, against the top of my head. I endure this terrible treatment without uttering a sound, preferring the worst suffering, even death itself, to losing my bet.
The Brahmin, bewildered, turns to my wife. He has barely brought the burning ingot near her feet when she begins to scream:
— I’ve lost! I’ve lost!
Then she says to me:
— Here, take your betel leaf…
I then explained to everyone present the bet we had made the night before.
— What! You let yourself be burned from head to toe for a single betel leaf! they exclaimed.
And that is why, ever since, everyone has called me Betel-Anantaya…
Once the laughter had died down, the judge declared this act of folly admirable and invited the last contestant to speak…

… and here is his story:
I had to go and fetch my young wife from her parents’ home.
My mother repeatedly warns me to behave properly and to say or do nothing that might reveal my foolishness. I promise to behave sensibly, set off on my journey and arrive at my in-laws’ home without incident.
As we are about to leave, my wife’s father showers us with blessings and sheds a flood of tears, as though he senses the disaster to come.
It is the summer solstice, and the heat is unbearable.
The scorching sand eventually burns the soles of my wife’s feet. She begins to cry, throws herself onto the ground and declares that she will not take another step.
I sit down beside her, not knowing what to do, when a merchant arrives.
Seeing my distress, he says:
— In this heat, your wife is going to die. Rather than stand helplessly by and watch her suffer her final agony, leave her with me. I’ll give you ten pagodas for her. That’s better than a corpse…
The merchant’s reasoning seems perfectly reasonable to me, and the price seems fair. So I hand over my wife.
He hurries away.
As for me, I arrive home with my feet roasted by the hot sand.
My mother, horrified to see me return alone, asks where my wife is and what on earth I have done this time.
I tell her everything that has happened since I left home.
She flies into a rage, calls me every name under the sun and ends up cursing the day I was born.
My wife’s parents learned what had happened to their daughter. They rushed to our house and would surely have beaten my mother and me senseless if we had not fled…
If my mother is to be believed, I really am the greatest fool of them all!
Once the laughter had died down, the judge solemnly declared that each Brahmin was just as foolish as the others, and that the soldier’s salute therefore belonged to every one of them.
Delighted with this decision, the four Brahmins went their separate ways, each shouting:
— I won! I won!
